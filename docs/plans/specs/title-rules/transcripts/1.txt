## The problem, as the record states it

**The rule**, `design@agent-skills@title-states-the-rule` with the shape fragment (`fragments/head-shape.md:44-55` and `:88-91`), holds four tests for a title:

1. "A title states a decision only while it is false of the nearest rival it beat."
2. It states the rule that admits members, not the list of members.
3. "A title states no more than its argument argues."
4. "The slug and the title stay aligned with the full scope of the decision."

**The tripwire** watched test 3. It was to fire if the reviews of two separate branches each found a title wider than its argument in a head their own branch wrote. Since it was rewritten on 2026-10-24, five merged branches had that finding: PRs #38, #39, #43, #57 and #73. Two of these I checked against the commit that wrote the head; the other three rest on what their review messages say. Example: in #38, the title of `design@agent-skills@component-split-signals` still named the nested Components, which its body no longer argued.

**The issue's suspected mechanism**: a session that moves a title from members to a rule picks its own words for the rule, and those words reach further than the argument the body carries.

## Why I don't think that is a problem with the decision

**Every instance was caught by the review of its own branch, and none reached main.** That makes the tripwire unsound by its own standard. Condition 1 of the tripwire standard says a cause becomes a tripwire only where the failure is silent, meaning no review reports it. All five were reported. What the firing shows is the review working, not the rule failing.

**What remains is cost, and it is real.** Since 2026-10-24:

- 246 `[review]` commits landed; 87 of them mention titles in their message (`git log --since=2026-10-24 --grep='^\[review\]'`, with and without `-i --grep=title --all-match`).
- 64 PRs merged in the same period.
- The findings go both ways: titles wider than the body, titles narrower than the body, and titles true of their rival.

## Your point on survivor bias

You're right, and it corrects one of my claims. "None reached main" is unverified. No pass of the design-record audit has run since the tripwire was written, so a title wider than its argument that a review missed would be invisible. All I can say is that none is known.

Your general point holds against the tripwire standard as written. Condition 3 says "A clause that counts what the decision instructs or endorses fires on the decision working". A review that reports a lapse is the workflow doing what it instructs. Yet the rule on slips still lets a clause fire "on the same failure recurring in separate pieces of work", even when every instance was reported. That is the inversion you describe: the more reliably reviews catch a lapse, the sooner the tripwire fires.

The signal that something is wrong is the opposite: a lapse that reached main with no procedure reporting it, found later by an audit, by another branch's review, or in use. That is also what the survivor bias hides, so it is exactly what a tripwire should watch.

**The pattern is not limited to the title tripwire.** At least three standing tripwires have a clause that counts review findings across branches (my script over the "Fires when" lines of every tripwires home):

| tripwire | the clause that counts reported findings |
| --- | --- |
| `tripwire@agent-skills@a-shortcut-decision-earns-a-head` | "a decision-record review finds … at instances in two separate pieces of work" |
| `tripwire@agent-skills@split-rule-from-exception` | "the reviews of two separate branches each find an exception split from its rule" |
| `tripwire@agent-skills@rejected-entry-cited-by-the-wrong-head` | "or the reviews of two separate branches …" |

Each also has a second clause, an audit finding the defect on main, which the change keeps. `tripwire@agent-skills@record-check-misses-a-head` also counts findings in two branches' reviews, but of a check that should have caught them earlier. That is a procedure failing, not a review working, so it stays as it is.

## On "if nothing was found, it would be worrying"

I'd argue against making zero findings a signal of its own. A run of reviews that find nothing has two causes: clean work, or blind reviews, and the count cannot tell them apart. The evidence that separates them is the same as above: a defect of the kind the review exists to catch, later found on main. A blind review shows up as exactly that.

So my proposal records your principle in the standard, and adds no separate watch on zero counts:

> A clause on how sessions follow an instruction fires only on an instance that reached the main branch with no procedure reporting it. Findings that reviews, checks or retrospectives report, however often they recur, show the procedure working, and fire nothing.
